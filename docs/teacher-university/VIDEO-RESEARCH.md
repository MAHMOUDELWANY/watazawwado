## C. Expert ideas we gained (our words), by module
- **Receptive skills (6).** Pick an interesting text at a comfortable level first. Raise interest and wake background knowledge with a picture, a quick task or questions. Pre-teach only the one or two words that would block the task. Set a clear **gist** task before reading or listening (checking predictions, or choosing the real title among plausible fakes). Give a time limit for reading (about the time it takes you to read it twice). **Peer-check** before class feedback: it builds confidence, gives speaking, and lets weaker learners hear a second answer. In feedback, nominate individuals so confident learners do not take over, and ask "why, where does it say that?". Do not write detail questions that repeat the text's wording, or learners just copy. Follow-up: speaking or writing on the topic, or look at the language inside the text. New trainers most often forget a clear task and the peer check. Jigsaw reading: each learner reads a different part, then they pool answers. Skimming is a light pass; scanning looks for one thing.
- **Checking meaning (4).** Seven ways other than "Do you understand?": real objects or pictures; physical action (do it); examples (what can you have as a pet?); timelines, but only when time is the issue; extension (finish the logical sentence); discrimination (what is the difference between X and Y?); personalisation (tell me something you used to do; works only if you know something about them).
- **Why correct (8).** Correction works: feedback is among the strongest teaching tools, and without it errors harden. Learners also expect and appreciate it: it shows you are listening and care about progress. But correcting everything, especially at low levels, silences them. The balance sits closer to "correct less" than most teachers feel.
- **What to correct (8), causes of errors:** not yet learned; the first language gets in the way (sounds, stress, intonation, forms that do not exist in L1, word order, false friends); over-generalising a pattern; slips under real-time pressure. If you teach learners from one language group, learn its typical traps.
- **How to correct (8):** ask who should fix it: the learner first, then peers, then you. Recast (say it back correctly) is gentle but learners may not notice; if they will not, be more explicit: repeat their error with a question tone, say "try again", use fingers, a word like "past tense?". For sounds, show the mouth. Rule of thumb: correct on the spot in the teaching and controlled-practice stages, and delay correction in free-speaking stages. After a fluency task, put 4 to 6 anonymous errors on the board, let pairs fix them. Anonymity lowers embarrassment and everyone benefits.
- **TBLT (3).** Four stages: pre-task (brainstorm a word web, pictures with questions, or a short model text), task preparation (clear directions, a checklist per group), the task itself (the teacher circulates quietly and takes notes in categories, no interrupting), post-task (address only the key points on the board, praise strong language too).
- **Writing (7).** Product approach: the finished text is marked, focus on form and accuracy. Process approach: planning, organising, writing, editing, revising; drafts count. Both are useful: teach learners how to use the process and how to deliver an acceptable product on demand. Components of a good product: audience, purpose, genre, language style, components, structure.

## D. Web research done 2026-11-17 (articles and studies, not video transcripts)
Sources are mostly practitioner pages and small studies; treat as supporting ideas, not proof. Findings in our words, each with a fit note for the group rule (language: full fit; Quran and Islamic sciences: check).

- **Eliciting.** Getting learners to produce the information instead of telling them; also a quick diagnostic of what they already know. Needs a starting stimulus (picture, object, retelling), real wait time, and praise or refining of half answers. Guard against a guessing game: if two prompts bring nothing, give the language and move to modelling and practice. Sources: British Council TeachingEnglish "elicitation"; teach-this.com; TKT study guide. *Fit:* all groups for meaning and examples (e.g. "what do you think this ayah is asking us to do?"); never for the text itself, which is given and recited, not guessed.
- **Guided discovery (deductive and inductive).** Studies mostly find the two approaches about equal for accuracy; guided induction tends to add motivation and strategy use. Caution: unguided discovery can lead learners to wrong rules, so give examples plus checking questions. Sources: comparative studies (German and Thai EFL, 2018 and later), ELT Notebook. *Fit:* languages fully. Tajweed rules can be discovered from examples in the mushaf, then confirmed by the teacher; religious rulings are never discovered by the learner, only explained.
- **Flipped lessons.** Pre-lesson task, live time for practice and feedback. Evidence for language learning is thin and mixed; risks are extra workload and learners skipping the prep; clear communication and tasks tied to the live lesson matter most; one-to-one fits because the teacher can fix a misunderstanding on the spot. Sources: Verbling, UK college OTLA report, Iranian and Estonian EFL studies. *Fit:* useful for hifz (listen and rehearse before the lesson, recite in the lesson) and for languages; keep prep short.
- **Principled eclecticism and post-method.** Kumaravadivelu's three parameters: particularity (fit this context), practicality (theory from classroom practice), possibility (learner identity and social relevance). "Eclectic" without principles degenerates into picking techniques at random; "principled" means you can say why. Primary sources: Kumaravadivelu 1994 TESOL Quarterly 28(1) and his 2006 book. *Fit:* all groups; it is the reason for the "applies, adapted, dropped" check.
- **Word and sentence stress (practical).** Teach stress with every new word, mark it on the board, groups by stress pattern, stress-pattern bingo, underline the stressed syllable, sentence stress by moving it to change meaning ("I like your car"), short pronunciation drills inside normal lessons rather than a separate block. Sources: TeachingEnglish, TEFL Academy, ESL Base, HLT Magazine, Busy Teacher. *Fit:* languages; the same discipline of short daily drills carries to makharij and tajweed (mouth shape, one sound at a time).
- **Genre-based writing.** Four stages: build knowledge of the topic, model a real text of the genre, write one together (joint construction), then write alone; support is withdrawn gradually. Studies: joint construction raised text length more than quality; many teachers use three stages; combining it with the process approach works for weaker writers. Sources: Victorian Dept of Education writing cycle, several Indonesian classroom studies. *Fit:* languages; the same scaffold (model, together, alone) fits teaching a child to write a short du'a or a Seerah summary.
- **Vocabulary revision and games.** One classroom study: reviewing after a week gave far better recall five weeks later than reviewing straight away (about 21% against 7.5%, a small single study). Retrieval beats re-reading; teach a small set, check with a low-stakes quiz, then add the next. Recycle words across topics; end lessons with a short game (team race on the board, "teach me in a minute", story-building where each learner adds a sentence with a target word). Sources: EdResearch for Australia, Oxford teaching blog, TEFL Academy, Language Angels. *Fit:* languages; for hifz and revision the spacing and retrieval logic is the same (new, near, far).
- **What learners say about teaching styles and correction.** Findings vary by context, level and error type. Some groups prefer teacher correction even while believing self-correction helps; others prefer self-correction; a mismatch between what the learner expects and what the teacher does creates dissatisfaction. Sources: Frontiers 2021 (Vietnam), Frontiers 2023 (Chinese as L2), F1000 2022 (Saudi), AJAL (Argentina). *Fit:* all groups: ask the learner at the start how they like to be corrected, and tell them how you will do it. Matches the "getting to know your students" module.

Still open: a primary-source check of Doff's *Teach English* and Kumaravadivelu 2006 before we quote them; Quran-specific teaching research (hifz methods, tajweed pedagogy) is not covered by this course and needs its own sources and the lead teachers' review.
