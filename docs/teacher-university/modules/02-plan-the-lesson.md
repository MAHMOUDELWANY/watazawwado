# Module 2: Plan the lesson and the course

**Why it matters.** You would not start a journey by walking in a random direction. A lesson without a destination feels busy but leaves the learner unsure what they gained.

## The ideas

### 1. Start from the destination: the lesson objective
Ask: *what should the learner be able to do by the end?*
A good objective has two parts:
- **A specific verb for a real action** (ask, describe, recite, explain, correct, compare), not "learn" or "talk about".
- **A context** where that action happens.

Weak: "Talk about wudu."
Better: "Describe the steps of wudu in order, and spot and fix one wrong step."

Weak: "Study the past tense."
Better: "Use the past tense to tell me what you did on the weekend, and ask me the same."

Rule of thumb: if you cannot picture the learner doing it, it is too vague.

### 2. Plan backwards
Decide the final task first, then plan the stages that make it possible. Ask:
- What must they know to do this task?
- What will help them practise it?
- Where will they likely struggle?

### 3. What goes in a plan
- Learner details and level
- Objective (one sentence)
- Materials: yours, the course's, or real material
- Procedure: each activity, its purpose, and how long it will take
- **Expected difficulties** in meaning, form and sound, and your answer to each
- **Personalisation**: how the activity connects to this learner
- **Feedback**: what you will note, and when you will give it

A plan can be half a page. The thinking matters more than the layout. (A template is in [templates.md](../templates.md).)

### 4. Share the destination
Tell the learner the objective in simple words at the start, and come back to it at the end: "Did we get there?" Learners who can see the goal stay more motivated and can tell you whether it worked.

### 5. Teach the learner, not the plan
A plan is a tool. If the learner raises a better question, or a stage is going badly, change the plan. Clear aims plus flexibility is the combination that works.

### 6. The course roadmap
When a learner (or a parent) can see what the next 8 lessons will cover, they stay with you. A good roadmap has:
- 2 to 3 goals in the learner's own words
- A line per lesson
- A review lesson and a way to show progress
- A note that it will change as you learn more

Check in regularly. Setting goals is a conversation that continues.

## In our lessons

- **Quran / Tajweed:** objective example: "Recite lines 1 to 5 of the passage with the ghunnah correct in each place it appears." Anticipated difficulty: rushing past the nasal sound. Plan the model, the isolated drill and the connected reading.
- **Hifz:** the plan has three blocks: new portion, recent revision, older revision. The plan names the exact lines for each.
- **Zad Path:** objective example (Seerah, adults): "Place the three events of the Hijrah in order and explain why each one mattered." Plan a short text, a question to predict, and a discussion.
- **Languages:** objective example: "Order food politely in Fusha, using three set phrases and one follow-up question."

## Try it
Write the objective of tomorrow's lesson using a specific verb and a context. Read it aloud. Could the learner picture it? If not, sharpen it.

## Check yourself
1. Which objective is stronger: "Learn the numbers" or "Give your phone number and ask for mine"? Why?
   *The second: specific action in a real context.*
2. What is "planning backwards"?
   *Starting from the final task and planning the steps that lead to it.*
3. Why predict difficulties in advance?
   *So you have an answer ready instead of improvising when they arrive.*
4. A stage is failing. What do you do?
   *Adapt: the plan serves the learner.*

## Reflect
What is one lesson you taught recently where a clearer objective would have changed what you did?
