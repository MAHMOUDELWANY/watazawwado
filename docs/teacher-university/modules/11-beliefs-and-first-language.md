# Module 11: Your beliefs, your style, and the learner's first language

**Why it matters.** Every teacher teaches from beliefs about how people learn. Most are never said aloud. Seeing them lets you keep what works and test what does not.

## The ideas

### 1. Common beliefs about learning
Teachers often hold views like:
1. Mistakes must be corrected at once, or they become habits (they "fossilise").
2. A plan is essential, but you adapt it.
3. It is better if learners work out rules than being told.
4. Imitating good examples and memorising whole phrases is how language sticks.
5. Without correct pronunciation, you do not truly speak a language.
6. Knowing the technical words (metalanguage) is needed to understand a language.
7. Games and activities boost learning.
8. Revision and homework are key to long-term memory.
Which do you hold? Which do you doubt? Each can be partly true. Beliefs can be read from what a teacher does: delayed correction suggests a belief in protecting fluency; lots of repetition suggests a belief in imitation and habit; many grammar exercises suggest a belief in rules as a backbone.

### 2. Learners tell you what they experience
Listen to how learners describe lessons: *"he never explains grammar, he gives many examples"*; *"she waits until the end to correct me"*; *"we repeat dialogues."* Ask yourself which teacher you would want, and why.

### 3. Principled eclecticism
There is no single best method. A strong teacher **chooses from several methods on purpose**, using a reason for each choice: this learner, this aim, this moment. "Principled" means you can explain why.
It is the opposite of using a favourite method for everything, and the opposite of random mixing.

### 4. The first language (L1)
Should teachers use the learner's first language? There are two honest views: keep the lesson fully in the target language for maximum exposure, or use translation as a helpful tool. A sensible middle:

**Do**
- Use L1 when it saves time or lowers stress, especially for beginners.
- Point out **false friends** (words that look alike but differ in meaning).
- Use comparison of languages and culture (idioms, for example) as a rich conversation.
- Make use of what you know about their first language to predict their errors.

**Do not**
- Explain every rule in L1 when the target language can do it with visuals, examples and questions.
- Translate every new word as a habit.
Use L1 deliberately, in small amounts, with a reason.

## In our lessons
- Our teachers are Arabic speakers teaching families whose first language is mostly English. For Fusha and Egyptian Arabic, English can lower stress for beginners; move toward Arabic as the learner grows.
- For Quran and Zad Path, the key texts stay in Arabic, with meaning explained in English that the learner understands. Never replace the text with a paraphrase.
- For English lessons, Arabic as L1 can explain false friends and sounds that the learner does not have.
- Where a belief touches religious knowledge, the curriculum and the senior teachers decide, not personal taste.

## Try it
Write three sentences that start "I believe learners learn best when...". Then look at your last lesson: did it match? Share one of them with a colleague and ask what they believe.

## Check yourself
1. What does "principled" mean in principled eclecticism?
   *Every choice of method has a reason.*
2. Give one good use of the first language.
   *Pointing out false friends, lowering stress for beginners, comparing cultures.*
3. What is a "false friend"?
   *A word that looks or sounds like a word in another language but means something different.*

## Reflect
Which belief of yours has most shaped how you correct and how you revise? Where did it come from?
