# Module 4: Make meaning clear (eliciting, checking, MFP)

**Why it matters.** Explaining a lot does not mean the learner understood. The skill is getting them to show you what they understand.

## The ideas

### 1. Eliciting: pull, do not push
Eliciting means asking questions so the learner brings out what they know or can guess. It is the opposite of lecturing.
- It shows you what they already know.
- It keeps them active.
- What they discover themselves is remembered better.
Examples: show a picture and ask what they call it; give a sentence with a gap and ask what fits; ask "what do you think this means?" before you say.

### 2. Ways to convey meaning
Pick the way by the word and the learner:
- Picture, object or gesture (concrete things)
- A short example sentence (most words)
- A definition (only if simpler than the word)
- A translation or L1 equivalent (used with care, see Module 11)
- A story or situation (abstract ideas)

A mime cannot show "productivity". A definition can be harder than the word. Match the tool to the word.

### 3. MFP: Meaning, Form, Pronunciation
For any new word, phrase or rule, cover three things, in this order:
1. **Meaning:** what does it mean here?
2. **Form:** how does it work? (which words go with it, how it changes, its grammar)
3. **Pronunciation:** how does it sound, where is the stress?
If the learner does not know how a word sounds, can we say they know it?
A colour code (one colour per area) on the shared screen helps memory.

### 4. Checking questions (CCQs)
"Do you understand?" is a poor check. Some will say yes to avoid embarrassment, and some believe they understood when they did not.
A **concept-checking question** asks something that can only be answered correctly if the meaning is clear.
Example, teaching "to borrow": *Does the person give it back? Who has it now? Do you pay for it?*
Plan two or three CCQs per target item before the lesson.

### 5. Functional language needs one more letter: A
Phrases for asking, refusing, advising, thanking (functions) have meaning and form, but also **appropriacy**: who is speaking, how well they know each other, how formal it is.
"You should go" and "It would be advisable to go" mean the same, but they do not belong in the same conversation. Always teach a function inside a clear situation.

### 6. Vocabulary: do and do not
- Do: let the learner make their own sentences; teach collocations (what it usually goes with); give many meaningful meetings with a word, not one.
- Do not: teach meaning, form and sound all before the learner has grasped the meaning; teach every sense of a word at once; skip pronunciation.

## In our lessons
- **Quran / Tajweed:** meaning and form become **meaning, rule and recitation**. For a word: what it means in the ayah, which rule applies to it, how it is recited. CCQ example: *"Where does the sound stop and for how many counts?"*
- **Zad Path:** a concept such as sabr or ihsan is built with a situation, then a CCQ: *"If a friend is rude and you stay calm, is that sabr? What if you stay calm because you are afraid?"*
- **Languages:** classic MFP on each new item. Always a clear context first.

## Try it
Choose three new words you will teach this week. For each, write one elicit question, two CCQs and a note on sound. Use them and write down which CCQ worked best.

## Check yourself
1. Why is "Do you understand?" unreliable?
   *Learners say yes from politeness or false confidence.*
2. What does the A in MFP+A stand for, and when is it needed?
   *Appropriacy; for functional phrases that depend on situation and formality.*
3. Which comes first: meaning or form?
   *Meaning.*
4. Give a CCQ for the word "to lend".
   *Does the person get it back? Who has it now?*

## Reflect
Which word did you recently "explain" for a long time? How could you have elicited it instead?
