# Module 1: Know your learner

**Why it matters.** Two learners book the same course. One wants to read the Quran without stumbling in front of her family. The other wants to finish Seerah with his kids before Ramadan. Same subject, two different lessons. Who is in front of you decides almost everything that follows.

## The ideas

### 1. Learner-centred means room, not silence
A learner-centred lesson is one where the learner does real work: answers, asks, chooses, tries. The teacher is a guide who knows the subject, not a tap that pours knowledge out.
Quick audit after any lesson:
- How much of the lesson was the learner active, and how much only listening?
- Did the learner ask a question that I did not prompt?
- Did they steer anything: a topic, an example, the order?

### 2. Teacher talk: the 3C check
What we say is the main input many learners get. Make it count.
- **Clear.** Short sentences, plain words, a pause between ideas.
- **Concise.** "Read the first line" beats "Could you possibly start by reading the first line for me?"
- **Checked.** Ask a small question to confirm they followed. Do not rely on "Does that make sense?"

Aim for the learner to talk more than you. In a 1-on-1 lesson that is a choice you make every minute.
Tip for newer teachers: script the two or three explanations you are least sure about. Saying them smoothly builds confidence fast.

### 3. Needs analysis: a conversation, not a form
At the first lesson (and again every few weeks) find out:
- **Why** they are learning and what "success" looks like in their words.
- **Level**: what they can already do (not what they say their level is).
- **Age and situation**: for children, talk to the parent and to the child, separately if you can.
- **Time**: lessons per week, practice time, a target date if there is one.
- **How they like to learn**, and what put them off before.
- **Interests**, so your examples can be theirs.

Needs change. Treat the analysis as something you update, not something you file.

### 4. Level: look for the edge
Ask questions that stretch a little past what they find easy: about the past, the future, an "imagine if" situation. The place where they hesitate is the edge of their ability, and that is where your lesson should live.
For languages we use the CEFR scale (A1 to C2). For Quran and Zad Path, use the level ladder defined in the subject's curriculum plan; do not invent your own.

### 5. Engagement has three parts
- **Thinking** (cognitive): the task is hard enough to be interesting and easy enough to finish.
- **Feeling** (emotional): it connects to their life, their goals, their reasons.
- **Doing** (behavioural): they take part, keep trying, come back next lesson.

Small habits that move all three: ask "why" and "how" instead of only "what"; let them predict or guess before you tell; celebrate small wins; give quick, specific feedback; let them type, mark or choose on the shared screen instead of you doing it.

## In our lessons

| Subject | What "know your learner" adds |
|---|---|
| Quran reading / Tajweed | Can they join letters? Which sounds are hard for them (often the ones their first language does not have)? Are they reading to pass an exam, or to feel at home in prayer? |
| Hifz and revision | Current memorised amount, how strong it really is, how much time at home, who listens to them at home. |
| Zad Path (kids, teens, adults) | Prior knowledge from home or Sunday school, attention span, what questions they already carry. |
| Fusha, Egyptian Arabic, English | Level on a clear scale, the situations they need the language for, the language they speak at home. |

## Try it
Take one instruction you often give. Write it as it comes out of your mouth. Now rewrite it with the 3C check: clearer, shorter, with a question that checks. Use the new version in your next lesson and note what changed.

## Check yourself
1. Name the three parts of engagement.
   *Thinking, feeling, doing (cognitive, emotional, behavioural).*
2. Why is "Does that make sense?" a weak check?
   *Learners say yes to be polite or because they think they understood. Ask something that shows it instead.*
3. A new learner is an adult beginner. Which three things do you ask first?
   *Why they are learning, what they can already do, how much time they have.*
4. True or false: a needs analysis is done once, in the first lesson.
   *False. It is revisited because needs change.*

## Reflect
Think of your last lesson. When was the learner most active, and what had you done just before that?
