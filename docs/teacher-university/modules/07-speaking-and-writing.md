# Module 7: Speaking and writing lessons (productive skills)

**Why it matters.** "Let's just chat" is not a lesson plan. Learners tell us they want structure and feedback, and they feel progress when they get both.

## The ideas

### 1. The conversation-first learner
Many learners mainly want to speak with more fluency and confidence. That does not mean they do not need rules or vocabulary. It means speaking is the centre of the lesson.
Three habits:
- **Use their interests.** Let them teach you about their topic.
- **Run a proper needs analysis** early, and revisit topic ideas.
- **Give feedback on what they said and on how they said it.**

### 2. React to what they say
When the learner says something:
- **Correct?** Celebrate it, openly.
- **Incorrect?** Decide: now or later (Module 8).
- **Correct but could sound better?** Offer an upgrade after they finish. This helps learning stick.
The more feedback they get, the more the lesson feels valuable, not just a chat.

### 3. A structure for a speaking lesson
1. **Lead-in:** open the topic with a few questions.
2. **Content preparation:** ideas first (a picture, a question, a short reading).
3. **Language preparation (optional):** useful phrases, with meaning, form and sound. Helpful for lower levels and for shy learners, because it lowers the mental load.
4. **Speaking task:** a role-play, a discussion, a problem to solve. Record it if you can.
5. **Content feedback:** what was said, was it successful?
6. **Language feedback:** good phrases, errors and upgrades.
7. **Repeat or extend:** do the task again with a small change. Repetition builds fluency and a sense of achievement.

### 4. A good speaking task
Personal and engaging, easy to reuse with small changes, needs little preparation from you, and produces plenty of speech from the learner.
While they speak, take notes on your side: good language and errors. Afterwards, put them on the shared screen. Ask the learner to decide which are correct and fix the rest.

### 5. Why writing needs teaching
Speaking is picked up by exposure. Writing usually is not; it needs instruction. A teacher adds: tailored feedback, quick clarification, structure and regular practice, and motivation.
A gentle trick on a shared screen: highlight the part to revise while they write, and they often self-correct.

### 6. How written language differs from spoken
Speech: shorter, hesitations, repetition, shared context. Writing: longer, more complex sentences, planned, more compact. A writing lesson must therefore be planned differently from a speaking lesson.

### 7. Genre
A genre is a type of text: an email, a story, a report. Each has its own structure, phrases and tone. Show real examples of the genre the learner needs, ask what they notice, then write.

### 8. Product or process
- **Product:** study a model text, practise its features, write a similar one.
- **Process:** plan, draft, get feedback, revise, edit.
Product suits short, fixed formats. Process suits longer or more personal pieces. Many lessons combine both.

## In our lessons
- **Quran:** speaking = recitation tasks with the same repeat-after-feedback shape; the "task repetition" step is exactly a second recitation after correction.
- **Zad Path:** speaking tasks: "explain this to a younger child", "what would you say in this situation?", a small debate for teens. Writing: a short reflection or a letter, using a model first.
- **Languages:** the full structure above; for writing, choose a genre the learner will really need.

## Try it
Run one speaking task in your next lesson using steps 4 to 7. Record the learner (with their permission) or take notes, and finish with one repeat of the same task. Compare the second attempt to the first.

## Check yourself
1. What are three things to do when a learner says something correct but plain?
   *Praise the content, offer a more natural alternative after they finish, invite them to reuse it.*
2. Why repeat a speaking task?
   *It builds fluency and confidence, and lets them use the new language.*
3. Name the two writing approaches.
   *Product and process.*
4. What is a genre?
   *A type of text with its own structure and language, such as an email or a story.*
