# Module 5: Teaching rules and patterns (grammar and tajweed-style rules)

**Why it matters.** If vocabulary is the bricks, rules are the mortar. Many learners have been taught rules badly: a table, a lecture, then drills. There is a better way.

## The ideas

### 1. Context first
The same words can say very different things depending on the form. A rule makes sense only when it is attached to a real situation. Teaching a rule with no context leads to confusion. Before explaining, ask: *when would someone really use this?*

### 2. Two filters: Efficient and Appropriate
Before you choose an activity, ask two questions.
- **Efficient?** Is it the quickest, clearest way to get this learner there?
- **Appropriate?** Is it relevant, and at the right level, for this learner?
Both must be yes.

### 3. Deductive or inductive?
- **Deductive:** present the rule, then examples that use it.
- **Inductive:** show examples, and let the learner work out the rule.
Use deductive for simple points or when the rule is close to the learner's first language. Use inductive (guided discovery) for harder points: give a little more time to notice and understand before applying and practising.
Neither is always better. Choose by the rule and by the moment.

### 4. Guided discovery in four steps
1. Give 3 to 4 clear examples in a context.
2. Ask noticing questions: *What is the same? What changes? What do you think the rule is?*
3. Confirm or refine the rule briefly.
4. Short controlled practice, then a free use.

### 5. A checklist for a rule lesson
1. The rule appears in a real, engaging context.
2. The activity is efficient and appropriate.
3. CCQs are ready for meaning and form.
4. At least two practice tasks (for example, matching, then gap-fill).
5. A speaking or writing task where the learner uses it for real.
6. You know the likely mistakes (meaning, form, sound) and how you will correct them.

### 6. Simple moves that work
- **Use examples:** write one or two sentences, ask what they notice, explain briefly, ask for 3 to 5 of their own.
- **Find out what they know first:** set a task that needs the rule. Listen. Teach only what is missing.
- **Situation picture:** use a picture to draw out the structure. Many items in one picture suit lower levels.

### 7. Do and do not
- Do: personalise; elicit; plan backwards; focus on the sound too.
- Do not: announce "today we do grammar"; pile on jargon; explain every use of a rule in one lesson.
Heavy technical vocabulary adds load without adding understanding. Use the word only if the learner needs it.

## In our lessons
- **Tajweed:** treat each rule as a pattern the ear can find before the mouth names it. Play the examples, ask what they hear, then name the rule. Practise in isolation, then inside a few ayat, then in free recitation. Anticipate the usual error (for example, rushing a held sound).
- **Zad Path:** a ruling or etiquette is taught the same way: a short situation, noticing questions, then the principle, then practice in a new situation. Where a ruling has scholarly differences, follow the curriculum plan; do not improvise.
- **Languages:** classic guided discovery for each grammar point.

## Try it
Take a rule you usually explain first. Rebuild it as guided discovery: write three examples and two noticing questions. Try it with one learner and note whether they reached the rule themselves.

## Check yourself
1. What is the difference between deductive and inductive?
   *Rule first and then examples, versus examples first and then the rule.*
2. Name the two filters for choosing an activity.
   *Efficient and appropriate.*
3. Why avoid starting with "today we do grammar"?
   *It is flat and does not connect the rule to a situation.*
4. Why might heavy terminology be a problem?
   *Extra mental load without extra understanding.*

## Reflect
Think of a rule that learners keep getting wrong. Could they have discovered it with the right examples?
